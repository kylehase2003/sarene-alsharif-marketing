# **How to Create the Right Content (What to Say, How to Say It)**

## **Section 1: Why Educational Content Still Wins**

Everyone assumes educational content is played out because information is free. It's not. Curation and lived experience are the scarce resource now.

**Curation beats access.** Anyone can Google a vacation. But a friend who just took the trip and tells you exactly what to book, what to skip, and what mistakes to avoid, that's who you actually listen to. Same with content: don't just hand over information, prescribe it.

**Hallway beats panel.** The main stage talk is generic, buzzword-y, predictable. The real insight happens in hallway conversations between practitioners trading what they're seeing right now.

**The dugout beats the record books.** Studying history teaches patterns. A player in the dugout right now, reading this pitcher, this weather, this field, that's real-time applied knowledge.

**Takeaway:** Don't compete on information. Compete on real-time, prescribed, lived experience.

---

## **Section 2: How to Generate Endless Ideas (Topic to Emotion)**

Most people run out of things to talk about because they're mining topics. Topics are finite. Emotions are not.

An idea starts as a topic. For example, "posting consistency."

Topics break into subtopics. Best time to post, batching, content calendars, repurposing. This is where most first ideas come from, and it works, for a while.

But subtopics run out. Once you've made those videos, you're circling back, sounding repetitive.

What never runs out is emotion: how people feel about the topic. The guilt of missing a day, the shame of comparing yourself to daily posters, the anxiety of being forgotten, the exhaustion of doing five jobs alone.

Emotion is who you're actually talking to. The topic is the doorway. The person on the other side wants to hear "I know why this feels impossible," not a schedule.

**Why this works:** Content's job is connection, not information transfer. General information fits everyone, so it resonates with no one. Specific pain points make the right person feel personally addressed.

**Contrast:**

**Topic-first:** "5 tips to post more consistently." Correct, forgettable.

**Emotion-first:** "You're not inconsistent because you're lazy, you're one person doing five jobs and burnt out by Tuesday." Same topic, completely different reaction.

**Takeaway:** When stuck, don't ask what's uncovered. Ask what this topic makes people feel, and whether you've said that part out loud yet.

## **Section 3: Say It Back, How to Form Opinions**

This is a practice for the time between videos: how you build a point of view you can actually put on camera.

Take a paragraph, video, or conversation.

Say it back out loud in one sentence. Saying it out loud is what flags it as important to your brain; silent paraphrasing doesn't work the same way.

Decide: do I agree or disagree?

Connect it to your own experience or a resource you know.

Repeating this moves you from passively consuming to actively forming opinions, which is what reads as "voice" on camera.

**Takeaway:** Compress, say it out loud, take a side. That's how opinions get made.

---

## **Section 4: The 3 Tests Before You Post**

Before publishing, run the script through three tests. Fail all three, and it's not a point, it's just information.

**The "I believe" test:** Is your position clear? Can the viewer tell exactly what you stand for?

**The "so what" test:** Is this arguable, or just a truism? Someone should be able to reasonably push back.

**The "why" test:** Does the viewer know why this matters to them? Fix it with the word "because."

"I think protein is the best supplement for men, because I'm not a fitness guy \[and even I know this\]."

**Takeaway:** A script needs a clear stance (I believe), a debatable edge (so what), and a stated reason (because).

# **Bridge: From Insight to Format**

You now know how to find a pain point (Topic to Emotion), turn it into a real opinion (Say It Back), and pressure test it (the 3 Tests). But an opinion sitting in your notes app doesn't help anyone, it has to go into something you actually shoot.

The reason people freeze here isn't lack of ideas, it's not knowing what to do with the idea once they have it: talk to camera? React to something circulating? Tell it as a story? Show it?

The pain point doesn't dictate the format. You choose the format based on how you want the viewer to experience it. Here's the menu, and the last one on it isn't really a format. It's a way of multiplying all the others.

---

## **Section 5: Content Formats**

**Talking-Head Take:** Straight to camera, one point, no gimmick.

**The Risky Text:** Quote a take everyone's repeating word for word on screen, then react to it directly enough it'd feel uncomfortable if the original poster saw it.

**The Substack Yap:** React to someone else's stance: state their position, agree or disagree and why, close with utility.

**Story-Time:** A single personal experience told start to finish, pain point embedded in the narrative.

**Voiceover Content:** Narrate your point over visuals (b-roll, images, screen capture, text on screen) so you're explaining verbally and showing it visually at once.

**Screen-Record / Walkthrough:** Show the actual thing (a tool, doc, process) while narrating the pain point it solves.

**The Debate Cut:** Stage or find a disagreement and referee it on camera.

Framework 2 specifically is how you turn any of those formats into long-form Yap content.

# **Framework 2: Master the Long-Form "Yap"**

Short clips are easy to copy. Long-form is harder to copy and the platforms favor it, but only if we can produce it consistently.

### **Two ways to do it:**

**Expand a proven short.** Take a listicle or short clip that already performed. Take each point and stretch it 20 to 30 seconds with a story, example, or insight. Stack the yaps together.

**Record real conversations.** Sit down with someone else in the niche for 10 to 15 minutes. This is where people show up naturally on camera, because they're talking to someone, not at a camera. Pull clips from the conversation, then repurpose the best moments into newsletters and LinkedIn posts, one conversation becomes a week of content across formats.

**Takeaway:** Proven short-form is raw material for long-form. One recording session, many outputs.

# **Framework 5: Three Video Formats to Produce**

Concrete formats the team can slot topics into:

### **The Substack Yap**

Say: "I read a \[substack/article\] about this."

State the author's stance.

Say whether you agree or disagree, and why, backed with points, ideally from your own experience.

Close with utility: how exactly can someone apply this?

### **The Risky Text**

Find an idea currently floating in the niche's zeitgeist, something that, if you said it directly to the person who posted it, would feel a little uncomfortable.

Quote the text word for word in the video.

Break down why it's wrong/bogus, backed by real examples from your life.

**Rule:** only disagree if you actually believe it, not disagreement for its own sake.

### **The Punch Bowl List**

Grab colorful objects, number them with a Sharpie, make a simple listicle.

Level up: support each point with a handwritten breakdown (whiteboard/notepad).

Don't worry about being copied, "it's the internet, they were going to either way."
