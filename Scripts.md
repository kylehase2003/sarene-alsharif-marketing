**1. "Employee Retention and Aligning Values."**

> If the only reason that your employees stay is because you pay them, you have a big problem. One survey found that 83% of Gen Zers are considered job hoppers. When you hear that, you shouldn't immediately be asking, "what's wrong with this generation?" Maybe the question should be, "what are we building that would make people want to stay?" I appreciate in the younger generations that they're understanding that we spend more time at work and with the people at work than we spend with our own families. So they understand the importance of finding value, and that their values align with the values of the business. They need to feel like they're making a difference, that the relationships they're creating with their team members or with their customers are valuable, and that they belong. And when we're able to create that sense of belonging, that's when we're more likely to get people to stay, because they see the value that they bring. Because it's so costly, whether it's money or time, if every few months you have to retrain somebody else. When you are able to align the values of your business and the paycheck with your team members' expectations, you actually save a lot of money. You get to keep that experience. You get to keep that person whose values are aligned with your business, and they become your biggest cheerleader. Instead of thinking about what is this employee doing for my business, maybe ask, how can I align my business with my team better?

**2. "Every Lock Has Its Own Key."**

> Do you use the same key for every lock? Probably not. Every lock has its own individual key, and that's how we are as human beings. Each one of us has a special key to open us up, and that is what we have to think of as leaders, and that's a mistake a lot of us leaders make. We try to treat everybody the way we want to be treated, or we give them the support that we want. But the reality is, a true leader knows that each one of their team members has a different lock, a different way to get to them. Somebody might need clear, specific directions. Another person might need safety, because they came from a background of trauma, and they need to know that you are a safe place. It's our job as leaders to know what the different people on our team need and support them as they grow, and know each individual lock, because at the end of the day, it's our job to help people grow and succeed, and it's one of the most rewarding parts of leadership.

**4. "Hiring Overlooked Candidates."**

> The person that you almost overlooked in the interview becomes the person that you can't imagine your team without. I think that happens because a lot of times we're looking for these certain skills, the person being outgoing and easily spoken. Well, not all positions require that. And some people bring amazing compassion, empathy, skills, and we might not give them the chance that they deserve. I believe a good leader is able to identify those strengths. So for example, somebody who has a wonderful technical skill but then isn't really good at working with people, or maybe they've just never been taught how to work with people, coaching them, praising the amazing skill that they have while coaching them so that they slowly, slowly grow, that to me is the most rewarding part, seeing the people that you work with grow over time and reach their true potential. And I'll just tell you a little secret: I don't think any of us will actually reach our true potential, because the more we learn, the more we grow, the higher our potential becomes. So I always believe that there's room for growth, and it's amazing to see the people around me growing, pushing themselves in ways that they never even thought that they would be doing.

**5. "Team Scenarios."** Q&A format, four leadership judgment calls.

> OFF CAMERA: Someone is incredible at their job but damaging to the team culture. What would you do?
> SARENE: They gotta go.
>
> OFF CAMERA: Someone on your team keeps making the same mistake despite your support. When do you stop giving them another chance?
> SARENE: First, I have to figure out why they keep making the same mistake. Is it training? Is it the situation? Once we determine the reason, if that can be fixable, then we're going to fix it. If there is no reason other than that person not wanting to do it right, then we have to have a different discussion.
>
> OFF CAMERA: You've given someone on your team the freedom to make decisions because you trust their judgment. Then they make a decision you strongly disagree with. What do you do?
> SARENE: This has happened multiple times, and nine times out of ten, when I ask the person why they made that decision and we discuss the details, I agree with them.
>
> OFF CAMERA: Someone you care about on your team is no longer performing at the level they used to. Do you give them more support or hold them to the same standard?
> SARENE: There's a reason that team member isn't performing, and figuring out the reason is the key. Maybe their time at the company is over, then I would help them find a different position. Usually that's not the case. Usually there's something else, whether it's personal or in our culture or in the job, and then helping them solve that so they can continue performing to their potential.

**6. "Values-Based Business Decisions."**

> Do you want to know if you have a values-based business? Building a values-based business means communicating those values to your customers and to your team. But really, what makes a differentiation between a values-based business and not, is how those values are changing the decisions that you're making. When you're making a decision to hire, to fire, which other businesses you're supporting, the vendors, the things that you're buying, if those values are not playing a role in those decisions, then really, you don't have a values-based business. You just have a list of preferences. Instead of asking yourself, "do I have a values-based business," the better question is, "what decisions are those values changing?"

**7. "Would You Rather."** Q&A format, four leadership trade-offs.

> OFF CAMERA: Would you rather have a team that trusts your judgment completely, or one that feels comfortable questioning you?
> SARENE: One that feels comfortable questioning me, because if they can question me, that means that they trust me.
>
> OFF CAMERA: Would you rather have someone who's amazing at what they do but doesn't fit your culture, or someone who's good at what they do and fits your culture?
> SARENE: Somebody who's good at what they do and fits the culture. I can't train culture fit, but I can train skills, so if they fit in and they embody our values, we can train the rest.
>
> OFF CAMERA: Would you rather have someone who needs a lot of support but has huge potential, or someone who needs very little support but may not grow much further?
> SARENE: Oh, potential, all the time.
>
> OFF CAMERA: Would you rather build a business that depends on you and works your way, or one that works differently but can grow without you?
> SARENE: One that works differently, but that can grow without me.
